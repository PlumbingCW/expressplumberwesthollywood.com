Water Heater Pressure Relief Valve in West Hollywood: When to Replace It

The temperature and pressure relief valve, usually called the T&P valve, opens when the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. It keeps a heater from becoming a hazard. A valve that drips, sticks or never moves is a warning.

This guide shows how to test the valve, what a leak means and how replacement works. One rule comes first: never cap or plug a relief valve.

What the valve does

The valve sits on the side or top of a tank heater and has a discharge pipe that runs down toward the floor. If the water gets too hot or the pressure passes the valve rating, it opens and releases water. That keeps the tank from over-pressurizing.

A valve is a mechanical part and it wears. Mineral deposits can stick it open or closed. A valve that never opens when needed is the dangerous failure, and you only find out by testing.

How to test the valve

Do this once a year. Keep your hand and face clear of the discharge pipe.

  1. 1
    Confirm a discharge pipe is attachedIt should end a few inches above the floor or drain.
  2. 2
    Place a bucket under the pipeWater may be hot.
  3. 3
    Lift the test lever brieflyWater should flow and stop when you release the lever.
  4. 4
    Watch for a drip afterwardA valve that keeps dripping needs replacement.

Drip causes and the right response

Not every drip is the valve.

PatternLikely causeResponse
Constant drip from the pipeWorn valve or debrisReplace the valve
Drips only when heatingThermal expansion pressureAdd an expansion tank
Spurts when hot water is usedPressure spikesTest pressure, check regulator
Wet around the valve baseLoose threadsReseal or replace
Steam or very hot waterOverheating tankShut off and call us now

Expansion tanks and high pressure

When water heats, it expands. In a closed system with a check valve or regulator on the supply, that water has nowhere to go, and pressure rises until the relief valve opens. An expansion tank gives the water somewhere to go. If your valve drips only after a heating cycle, ask about one.

The California Plumbing Code calls for a regulator when static pressure is above 80 psi. High incoming pressure makes expansion problems worse. We test both before replacing the valve, because a new valve in a high-pressure system just starts dripping again.

Replacement: what a plumber does

We shut off the heater and the water supply, drain a little from the tank and unscrew the old valve. A new valve with the right pressure and temperature rating goes on with proper thread sealant. The discharge pipe is checked for material, slope and an open end. Then the tank is refilled, the air is purged and the valve is tested.

The discharge pipe matters. It should be a material approved for hot water, with no valve or cap on the end. Many older installations have a pipe that is too small, has a trap in it or points up. Correcting it is part of a proper job. For a heater that is also leaking at the tank, see water heater repair.

Never do these

A relief valve is a safety device.

  • Never cap or plug the dischargeThe valve cannot protect the tank if it is blocked.
  • Never replace with a lower-rated valveThe rating must match the tank.
  • Never ignore steam from the pipeShut off the heater and call a plumber.
  • Never stand in front of the dischargeWater may be hot enough to scald.

Signs the valve itself is the problem

A valve that weeps a few drops after every heating cycle is wearing out. A valve with a white crust around the outlet has scale inside. A valve that has never been lifted in ten years may be stuck in the closed position, which is the more dangerous case, because the tank has no relief if pressure rises.

If the lever feels frozen, do not force it. A broken lever leaves you with a valve that cannot be tested at all. Call a plumber and have the valve replaced, which is a modest job compared with the risk of a heater that cannot vent pressure.

What a good discharge pipe looks like

The pipe should run straight down from the valve to a point a few inches above the floor or a drain, with no cap, plug or valve on the end. It should be a material approved for hot water, the same size as the valve outlet and free of traps and sharp bends.

A pipe that dead-ends in a wall or a bucket hides a drip until the floor rots. Where there is no floor drain, many installers run the pipe to the outside or to a safe termination. Check that the end is visible, since a hidden drip is the one you never fix.

Local rules that touch your heater

California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires water heaters up to 120 gallons to be braced, anchored or strapped, including new and replacement units. A relief valve discharge pipe and a seismic strap are often checked together in an installation. If your heater sits in a closet or garage in an older building, look for the straps while you test the valve.

Water hardness changes how fast a valve sticks. Beverly Hills Water reports about 290 mg/L, roughly 17 grains per gallon, and LADWP reports a range by source. Scale is the usual reason an old valve will not reseat. Our Beverly Hills page and Hollywood page describe what we see in those areas.
